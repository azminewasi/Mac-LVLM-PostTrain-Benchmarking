Post-training Qwen3.5 on a 16 GB MacBook: what works, what breaks

November 17, 2026 · Apple M5 MacBook Pro, 16 GB · MLX · 4 of 5 started runs finished · work in progress

The question

Our companion post measured how far a 16 GB MacBook goes at running Qwen3.5. This one asks the harder question: how far does it go at training it?

Running a model streams weights through the chip once per token. Training does that and more: it keeps activations for the backward pass, gradients, optimizer state, and for reinforcement learning (RL) it also has to generate whole batches of answers before it can learn from them. On a laptop where macOS alone takes about 6.5 GB of the 16, each of those costs matters.

We set out to compare four post-training methods on the same task: SFT (imitate reference solutions), and three critic-free RL methods, GRPO, RLOO and GSPO (learn from the model's own answers, rewarded when correct). Each is tried with LoRA adapters on the 0.8B, 2B and 4B models, and with full fine-tuning on the 0.8B. The task is GSM8K grade-school math: 500 training questions for 5 epochs, scored on 100 held-out test questions, with one line per (model, method, adapter) in Weights & Biases and every rollout kept.

This is a work-in-progress report: it regenerates from whatever has finished. Currently running: 0.8B GRPO full (step 0/625). What it already contains is a good deal of what broke on the way, which turned out to be the most informative part.

The short version so far

  • Memory is the first wall, and it comes much sooner than for inference. A single 0.8B training step peaked at 15.8 GB before we turned on gradient checkpointing, and 6.9 GB after.
  • Training required re-implementing a layer. Qwen3.5's fast attention kernel has no gradient; the library fallback was 50x too slow.
  • Three separate hyperparameter and reward mistakes each wrecked a run, and each looked like "the method doesn't work" until we read the curves.
  • SFT on 500 human solutions makes this model worse. On the 0.8B, LoRA SFT took accuracy from 54% to 22% after five epochs (it was 17% after the first and peaked at 29% in between), while the training loss fell to about 0.01.

1. The setup, in plain words

  • SFT: show the model a reference solution and train it to reproduce it token by token.
  • GRPO: sample 4 answers per question, reward the correct ones, and nudge the model toward answers that did better than their group average.
  • RLOO: same sampling, but each answer is compared to the average of the other answers for that question (a leave-one-out baseline), with a plain policy gradient.
  • GSPO: like GRPO, but the update is weighted at the whole-sequence level instead of per token.
  • LoRA trains small adapter matrices (10.8M parameters on the 0.8B) on top of frozen weights; full fine-tuning updates every transformer weight and needs fp32 master weights and Adam state (about 14 bytes per parameter).

All RL methods here are critic-free and have no reference-model KL penalty, so only one model sits in memory. Each RL step uses 4 prompts x 4 rollouts at temperature 1.0 and up to 640 new tokens. The learning rates are 2e-5 (SFT, LoRA), 1e-5 (SFT, full), 2e-6 (RL, LoRA) and 1e-6 (RL, full). The 0.8B runs use the full 500 samples x 5 epochs; the 2B and 4B use LoRA for 1 epoch to fit the time budget.

2. The first thing that broke: no gradients through Qwen3.5

Qwen3.5 is not a plain transformer. Most of its layers are "gated delta" linear-attention layers, and MLX ships a fast Metal kernel for them that has no backward pass.

The first training step crashed with Primitive::vjp not implemented for CustomKernel. The library's pure-operations fallback works, but it loops over tokens in Python: 50 seconds for one step on two short sequences, which would have made the whole project impossible. We wrote a chunk-parallel, matmul-based version of the recurrence (pt/gdn.py) that autograd can differentiate. It went through two bugs on the way (a numerically unstable matrix inverse that produced garbage on real, correlated keys, replaced by forward substitution) and now matches the reference: 4% relative error in the gradients on the real model, the same loss at step 1 (1.09 on both paths), and about 1 second per SFT step.

Takeaway. On Apple silicon, "can I fine-tune model X?" can depend on whether every layer type has a backward kernel. Check before you plan.

3. SFT: imitation made the model worse

On the 0.8B, LoRA SFT took accuracy from 54% to 22% after five epochs (it was 17% after the first and peaked at 29% in between), while the training loss fell to about 0.01. The model memorised the 500 reference solutions and lost the stronger, more verbose reasoning style it already had.

Our first attempt, at a learning rate of 1e-4, was far worse: accuracy collapsed from 54% to 4% with degenerate repeated output. LoRA with scale 20 is very sensitive; 2e-5 trains stably but still lowers accuracy. A reasonable reading: Qwen3.5 already reasons well in its own style, and 500 terse human-written solutions over five epochs pull it toward a style it cannot execute as well. That is a finding, not a bug: it is exactly the situation RL is supposed to improve on, because RL trains on the model's own outputs.

4. RL: two ways to teach a model to stop thinking

Both of our early GRPO failures were the same disease: the model found a way to get reward without being correct, or was pushed too hard by noise.

Reward hacking

The first GRPO run collapsed from 54% to 1% accuracy within the first epoch. The reason is in the reward. We capped generation at 400 tokens, and this model is verbose: 44% of rollouts were truncated and scored 0. A short, wrong answer in the right format earned a 0.1 format bonus, which beat 0. Within 50 steps the policy had learned that skipping the reasoning pays: completion length fell from 288 to about 35 tokens and every group looked the same. Fix: reward correctness only (the format flag is logged but not rewarded) and allow 640 tokens.

Learning-rate drift

The second attempt no longer collapsed in length, but reward and format rate drifted down steadily (0.42 to 0.1 and 0.89 to 0.39 over 25 steps) at a learning rate of 5e-6. With only 16 rollouts per step, and about half the groups carrying no signal, small noisy updates accumulate quickly under LoRA scale 20. At 2e-6 the run is stable. We verified the gradient code against the reference before blaming the hyperparameters.

5. What changed besides accuracy

Accuracy is one number. The answers themselves change too: their format, length and style, and some of those changes are side effects nobody asked for.

The clearest example is the answer format. Our prompt ends with "End with a final line: 'The answer is: <number>'", and the untrained model follows it almost always. During RL we stopped rewarding that format (after it had been exploited, chapter 4). The result: in the sampled training rollouts the share ending with "The answer is: N" fell from about 0.8 to about 0.13 over the first two to three epochs, and on the greedy test answers it fell from 86% to 56% by epoch 2 (so far, in the GRPO run), while accuracy did not suffer, because the evaluator reads the last number in the answer. The model silently stopped following a formatting instruction that nothing rewarded any more. That is harmless for this benchmark and could be a real problem in a pipeline that parses the format. GRPO also gradually dropped LaTeX (80% of answers at epoch 0, 36% at epoch 2) while keeping its markdown headers.

SFT changes the style far more. After SFT the model stops writing the long, markdown-heavy, LaTeX-heavy step-by-step answers it started with (95% and 80% of answers) and writes terse plain-text solutions like the GSM8K references: mean length falls from 347 to about 90 tokens and markdown and LaTeX disappear entirely. It follows the "The answer is:" format more reliably (99-100%), but gets fewer answers right, and in the first epochs it sometimes degenerates into a repeating arithmetic loop that runs to the token cap (see the example below). So the SFT story is as much about style as about accuracy: the model traded its own, stronger way of reasoning for a shorter one it executes worse.

6. Memory: the wall comes sooner

A training step that peaked at almost 16 GB on a model with 1.6 GB of weights.

While the second GRPO attempt was running, system swap climbed to 5 GB and step times swung between 11 and 46 seconds. Profiling one forward and backward pass explained it: without gradient checkpointing, the activations of the 0.8B for a batch of two 800-token sequences needed almost the entire 16 GB. Turning on checkpointing (recomputing activations in the backward pass) cut that to 6.9 GB at the cost of some speed, and the restarted GRPO run peaks at 6.8 GB so far.

This is the number to carry to the larger models, and it is not reassuring. Even with checkpointing, the 0.8B needs about 5 GB of working memory on top of its 1.6 GB of weights. The 4B has 8.5 GB of weights and bigger activations, so as configured it very likely does not fit in the ≈9.5 GB we have: it would need a micro-batch of 1, shorter sequences, or a more memory-efficient backward pass for the gated-delta layers. We will measure the 2B first and decide whether the 4B runs are feasible before spending days on them.

7. What a run costs in time

SFT is cheap; RL is generation-bound and slow.

An SFT step (4 sequences) takes about a second, so five epochs of 500 questions finish in roughly 15 minutes. An RL step must first generate 16 answers of up to 640 tokens at decode speeds of a few hundred tokens/s, then train on them: 15-45 seconds on the 0.8B. A 625-step RL run is therefore several hours, and the 2B and 4B are roughly 2.5x and 5x slower still, which is why those use one epoch.

8. So what can this MacBook train?

Small models, comfortably, with LoRA; the full fine-tune of even the smallest model is a stretch.

  • Budget: macOS takes ≈6.5 GB, leaving ≈9.5 GB for weights, gradients, optimizer state, activations and (for RL) the generation cache.
  • LoRA is the practical route. The adapter is ~11M parameters, so optimizer state is negligible; the real costs are the frozen weights and the activations (use gradient checkpointing and micro-batches of 1-2).
  • Full fine-tuning needs about 14 bytes per parameter (bf16 weights, fp32 master copy, two fp32 Adam moments): ≈11 GB for the 0.8B, so it only fits the smallest model and leaves little room for activations. Larger models are out of reach for full fine-tuning.
  • RL is slow, not impossible. The cost is generation time, which is set by memory bandwidth exactly as in the inference post.
  • Bigger models: a 9B would need LoRA on a 4-bit base; we did not try it.

9. Conclusions so far

  1. A 16 GB MacBook can post-train small Qwen3.5 models, but you must budget for activations, not just weights. Gradient checkpointing changed a 15.8 GB step into a 6.9 GB one.
  2. Check backward support layer by layer. Qwen3.5's hybrid architecture needed a custom differentiable kernel before anything trained.
  3. SFT on a small human-written set hurt a model that already reasons well. More data or a gentler recipe would be needed for it to help.
  4. RL needs a careful reward and a small learning rate. Reward that can be earned without being correct will be found within 50 steps; watch reward and length together.
  5. Unrewarded behaviour drifts. After we stopped rewarding the "The answer is:" format, the share of answers using it fell from ~0.8 to ~0.13 without hurting the score. Track style, not just accuracy.
  6. Time scales brutally with model size for RL. Plan for hours per run on the 0.8B and days for the larger models.

What we did not measure, and caveats

Test accuracy is on 100 questions (about ±10 points) with one seed per setup; learning rates were corrected after the failures above rather than swept, so the method comparison is not a tuned head-to-head; power draw and batteries are untested.

C. Method and data notes

  • Data: 500 GSM8K train questions (seeded subset saved with each run); evaluation on the first 100 test questions, greedy, thinking off, 512-token cap.
  • Hyperparameters: LoRA rank 16, scale 20, no dropout; gradient clipping 1.0; gradient checkpointing on for all runs except the first SFT run (0.8B LoRA), which finished before checkpointing was enabled for the 0.8B (it only changes memory and speed, not the maths); seed fixed.
